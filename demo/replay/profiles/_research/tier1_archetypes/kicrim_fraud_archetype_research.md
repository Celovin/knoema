# KICRIM Fraud Archetype Research

## Overview

The KICRIM fraud archetype is a no-person pedagogical profile for economic and cyber-financial deception patterns in South Korea. It is not a profile of a named offender and must not be used to infer real suspects. The profile exists because the replay viewer needs one non-violent criminology example that can show planning, target suitability, and memory-state changes without graphic public-safety content. The source base combines Korean government research on technology-enabled financial fraud, peer-reviewed work on fraud harm in South Korea, comparative policing work, and routine activity theory. In the viewer, the profile should appear as an overlay that manipulates trust cues, timing, and commercial-grid contact opportunities while avoiding instructions for committing fraud.

## Primary sources

The closest primary source is the Korean Institute of Criminology and Justice report on new methods of cyber-financial fraud and policy response [C1]. It is a government research report, not a court file, and it describes policy context, institutional overlap, victim-reporting barriers, and technology-enabled diversification. National Police Agency statistics are referenced through peer-reviewed and policy sources rather than copied into the YAML [C2]. The profile should treat these materials as policy background. It must not include scripts, message templates, payment instructions, or technical bypass detail.

## Academic secondary sources

Kim, Park, Lee, and Cho provide a recent peer-reviewed study of fraud crime and older-adult suicide rates in South Korea, including discussion of increasing sophistication and older-adult vulnerability [C2]. Button and Cross's comparative policing note places South Korea beside England and Wales and emphasizes cyber fraud as a modern policing issue [C3]. Jung and Kim's insurance-fraud article supplies a Korean economic-crime example with policy implications [C4]. Cohen and Felson provide the convergence framework [C5]. Lee, Park, and Jung provide Seoul CPTED context for built-environment guardianship, although the fraud archetype uses social and informational guardianship more than physical lighting [C6]. These sources are enough to build a cautious, non-operational, educational fraud profile.

## Behavioral analysis

### Victim profile

The profile should use target criteria such as low informational guardianship, high trust in institutional cues, and time pressure. It should not encode age as a targeting rule in the live simulation, even though older adults are discussed in the fraud-harm literature [C2]. The safe formulation is "low verification support" rather than demographic vulnerability. In a classroom, the instructor can explain why victim-blaming is avoided: fraud harm emerges from offender deception, social trust exploitation, and institutional gaps, not from a victim's character.

### Modus operandi

MO rules must be declarative and abstract: create legitimacy cue, request attention, wait for response, abort when verification support appears, and leave an audit trace. The YAML must not include phishing text, impersonation scripts, payment rails, malware references, or evasion details. The KICJ report supports the broad claim that technology-enabled fraud has diversified and become harder to detect [C1]. The replay translates that into trust-state changes and guardian-state checks, not operational content.

### Signature elements

No signature behavior should be encoded. Fraud may involve repeated scripts or styles, but reproducing them would create misuse risk. The profile should instead include a "policy teaching" signature: the agent visibly fails when a guardian node, verification cue, or peer check is present. This lets evaluators see how Knoema can model prevention interventions without teaching fraud.

### Geographic behavior

Fraud is not primarily tied to alley or street geometry. In the Gangnam-style grid, the archetype should prefer commercial and transit cells because they represent high message exposure, service encounters, and time pressure. The mobility radius can be broad, but interaction attempts should be blocked by "verification guardian" markers. This maps routine activity concepts to informational space: target suitability, motivated actor, and absence of capable guardianship [C5].

### Temporal patterns

The profile should peak during commute and retail transition moments. The reason is pedagogical: distracted movement and service transactions create visible opportunity states. It should not claim that real fraud incidents peak at 7 p.m. unless a future source is added. The replay should mark all temporal rules as synthetic.

### Victim-offender interaction dynamics

The interaction model should be "trust cue offered, verification requested, cue fails or disengages." The viewer panel should show this as labeled memory rows, not as raw JSON or message scripts. This keeps the artifact safe for grant evaluators and criminology faculty.

## Geographic profiling data

There is no geographic profiling dataset for this fraud archetype. The relevant spatial concept is opportunity distribution across commercial and transit-like cells. Routine activity theory is used as the event model [C5], while the KICJ and policing sources provide policy context [C1, C3]. The profile's movement rules are synthetic: commercial-cell preference, wide mobility, high sensitivity to verification guardians, and no physical convergence event. If future work adds measured fraud-location data, it should live in a separate profile with explicit consent and policy review.

## Contemporary Seoul translation

Gangnam's commercial density and modern urban identity make it a readable demo environment [C7]. The profile maps "commercial strip" to service interaction, "main road" to hurried movement, and "guardian" to both visible patrol and verification support. Seoul CPTED literature is physical, but its broader point is that environmental design and guardianship can change perceived safety and behavior [C6]. The fraud profile extends that idea into informational guardianship: peer confirmation, institutional warning, and delayed response.

## Ethical considerations

Fraud profiles can easily become instructions. This one must be defensive and abstract. It must not include real scripts, named institutions, current scam variants, payment pathways, or technical exploitation steps. The YAML should also avoid demographic targeting and victim-blaming. The ethical disclaimer should say that the profile is for teaching prevention, guardianship, and social-trust dynamics. It is Tier 1 only and has no real person reference.

Implementation should make the prevention mechanism more visible than the deceptive mechanism. The viewer can show a trust cue as a generic state, but it should show the guardian response in greater detail: peer verification, institutional warning, cooling-off delay, and audit trace. This design choice prevents the demo from functioning as a fraud playbook. It also aligns the profile with grant and classroom goals, where the interesting question is how an agent architecture can model safety interventions and memory updates.

The profile should not treat older adults or migrants as inherent targets. Some cited research discusses older-adult harm because that is a real policy concern, but encoding age-based targeting would be ethically wrong and unnecessary. The YAML should instead encode "low verification support" and "high trust cue acceptance" as situational states that can apply to any synthetic agent. That language shifts the lesson toward institutions and guardianship.

The contemporary Seoul translation should also be source-limited. Cyber-financial fraud is not literally tied to a Gangnam alley cell, so the grid is an interaction canvas, not a geography claim. Commercial cells stand for service encounters and attention pressure. Main-road cells stand for hurried transitions. Guardian cells stand for verification support. The profile should not imply that a particular district, store type, or institution is associated with fraud. The result is a safe, inspectable overlay that lets stakeholders discuss prevention without operational detail.

The final YAML should include a hard "no scripts" rule. If future users want to test fraud-prevention dialogue, they should do it in a separate red-team environment with explicit safety review and synthetic messages designed to be non-deployable. This public replay should stay abstract.

The viewer should also avoid urgency language that resembles real fraud pressure. Button text, memory rows, and exported CSV labels should use neutral terms such as "verification cue," "trust cue," and "audit trace." This preserves the teaching value while making it hard to repurpose the artifact as a persuasion script. The profile's success condition is not a completed deception; it is a visible prevention interruption when guardianship appears.

That prevention-first framing is the profile's central acceptance constraint.

It should remain visibly defensive.
