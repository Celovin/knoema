# FBI Disorganized Archetype Research

## Overview

The FBI disorganized archetype is a no-person reference profile derived from the same behavioral-science tradition as the organized profile. In the historical typology, disorganization is associated with opportunistic action, poor planning, local familiarity without structured search, chaotic post-event behavior, and weak evidence management. For the Luvoire replay, it must be represented without graphic detail and without implying that mental illness, poverty, social awkwardness, or neurodivergence predicts violence. The strongest finding for this educational use is actually a limitation: the organized/disorganized split is contested, and disorganized features may be too rare or too mixed to support a clean category. The replay should therefore present the profile as a disputed teaching construct, useful for discussing typology problems.

## Primary sources

The originating operational material is the FBI sexual homicide research program and later classification manuals [C1, C2, C3]. These sources were built from offender interviews and investigative case material, not from controlled experiments. They are important because they explain how the disorganized category entered training and courtroom language. They are also limited because the interview sample and variable coding have been criticized. The replay should cite them only as the historical source of the typology. It should not claim that the category has strong predictive validity, and it should not add details from sensational popular crime accounts.

## Academic secondary sources

The central secondary source is Canter, Alison, Alison, and Wentink's empirical critique, which found no distinct organized/disorganized split in their multidimensional scaling analysis [C4]. Kocsis, Cooksey, and Irwin's arson study is useful because it tests whether organization concepts generalize outside the original homicide setting [C5]. The Cambridge Handbook chapter frames profiling as probabilistic inference that must be tested [C6]. Rossmo's geographic profiling monograph gives a separate, more spatially grounded vocabulary for mobility and anchor points [C7]. Cohen and Felson supply the opportunity framework used to translate the archetype into simulated convergence events [C8]. Together these sources support a cautious model: local, opportunistic, noisy movement, but no deterministic person-level inference.

## Behavioral analysis

### Victim profile

The replay should not assign demographic victim preferences. Historical descriptions of disorganized scenes sometimes imply immediate availability and low planning, but the empirical literature does not justify projecting that into a contemporary Seoul demo. The safe target criteria are purely situational: sudden proximity, low visibility, and no capable guardian within one cell [C8]. This keeps the profile anchored in opportunity theory rather than stigmatizing people or places.

### Modus operandi

MO rules should be minimal and abstract: wander, notice convergence, approach abruptly, abort when a guardian appears, and return to a familiar anchor. The original FBI description used richer crime-scene language [C1, C3], but that detail is unnecessary and unsafe for an offline stakeholder replay. Canter's critique also means the rules should be labeled as a disputed typology translation [C4]. The agent can exhibit inconsistent pathing and short decision horizons without any operational content.

### Signature elements

No signature behavior should be encoded. The profile's educational purpose is to contrast planned and unplanned movement in a synthetic grid, not to dramatize harm. Any expressive or graphic detail would be outside the scope of this repository. A negative YAML rule should explicitly prohibit signature simulation.

### Geographic behavior

Disorganized movement should be close to an anchor point and less optimized than the organized profile. Rossmo's work supports using anchor point and distance-decay language, while warning that geographic profiling is a heuristic that depends on adequate event data [C7]. In the 20 by 20 grid, the profile can have a 1-3 cell radius around a familiar alley or transit-edge anchor. It should sometimes drift but should not cross the full grid with strategic loops.

### Temporal patterns

The profile should be sensitive to immediate crowd thinning rather than a long pre-planned schedule. Evening commute creates changing guardianship and target density. Routine activity theory supports modeling crime-event risk as convergence in time and space [C8]. The simulated profile should make abrupt choices at ticks where the local cell changes, not at a detailed clock schedule.

### Victim-offender interaction dynamics

Interaction should be short, unstable, and visible as a failed social approach or retreat. The viewer panel can describe "aborted approach" or "withdraws after guardian appears." It must not include coercive scripts or procedural language. The point is to show that guardianship and environmental design alter simulated path outcomes.

## Geographic profiling data

There is no case-specific geographic profile for a no-person disorganized archetype. The available quantitative point is negative: the organized/disorganized model itself has weak cluster support in Canter's analysis [C4]. For replay purposes, the geographic parameters are deliberately synthetic: one anchor point, 1-3 cell radius, high local repeat rate, and low route optimization. The report should label these as teaching parameters. If a future instructor wants empirical distance distributions, they should add a separate, cited dataset rather than treating the archetype as a data source.

## Contemporary Seoul translation

The Seoul mapping should use built-environment concepts rather than personal pathology. Gangnam's commercial blocks and side-street interiors can be represented as high-flow edges with lower-flow interior cells [C9]. Seoul CPTED research supports visualizing lighting, maintenance, CCTV, and walking behavior as environmental conditions that influence perceived safety [C10]. Therefore the profile should become less active near bright main-road cells and more likely to drift in interior cells where the heatmap shows thinning movement. This maps the teaching idea to environment and guardianship, not to a real local offender.

## Ethical considerations

The disorganized label is especially prone to misuse because it can be confused with mental disorder, low income, or social marginality. The profile must avoid diagnostic language. The viewer should show the ethical disclaimer before injection and should make the contested validity visible. It should also state that no contemporary or Korean named case is included. The safe teaching frame is "how typologies can mislead when treated as person classifiers." The YAML should include literature sources that show both the originating theory and the empirical critique.

Implementation should make uncertainty visible in the user interface. The profile panel should use labels such as "disputed typology," "synthetic movement parameter," and "no person inference." It should not use medicalized labels, intelligence labels, housing-status labels, or language that would convert disorder into a visual trope. The movement rules can be noisy, but the narrative must remain careful: the agent is not disorganized because of a diagnosis; it is disorganized because the teaching model is demonstrating short-horizon decisions and low route optimization.

The profile should also be constrained by the replay's safety purpose. It may show wandering, abrupt route changes, and retreat after guardianship appears. It should not show graphic harm, forensic aftermath, or instructional concealment. Any event generated by the runner should be named "unguarded convergence" or "aborted convergence" rather than a criminal act. That naming keeps the classroom discussion on opportunity structures and prevention. It also prevents the demo from implying that a synthetic trace is a prediction about real streets.

Because the empirical critique is central, the YAML should cite Canter et al. next to the originating FBI sources rather than burying the critique in a bibliography. The viewer can surface a one-line caution: "This archetype is historically important and scientifically contested." That sentence matters because stakeholders may otherwise treat the profile library as an authority layer. The model should invite critique and source inspection. If a future user wants a more empirically grounded profile, they should add a dataset-specific profile and keep this one as a historical comparison.

The committed version should therefore remain deliberately modest and auditable.
