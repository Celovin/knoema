# Whitechapel 1888 Historical Profile Research

## Overview

Whitechapel 1888 is a Tier 2 historical canonical profile for the unidentified offender commonly called Jack the Ripper. The case belongs to public-domain Victorian history, but it is also mythologized and disputed. The replay must use the profile only as a criminology teaching overlay, not as an attempt to identify a person. The relevant historical frame is a series of unsolved murders in London's East End in 1888, investigated by Metropolitan Police and City of London Police files. The behavioral frame is limited to spatial movement, alley density, victim exposure, and guardianship. Named suspect theories are excluded. The profile translates a historical urban environment into a 2026 Seoul-style grid by mapping alley networks, transient footfall, and public-place guardianship to synthetic cells.

## Primary sources

Primary sources include Metropolitan Police Office files MEPO 3/140 and MEPO 3/141, including Whitechapel murder reports, police memoranda, and contemporary correspondence [C1]. The National Archives digitized items from MEPO 3/141, including documents dated November 1888 [C1]. Contemporary newspapers, such as press reports collected in archival casebooks, are primary only when treated as period reporting rather than confirmed fact. Police files are incomplete and shaped by Victorian investigative limits. The profile must therefore avoid confident claims about identity, medical skill, or exact motive. Primary-source details should support only broad movement and setting features: public streets, lodging-house districts, late-night movement, and dense urban poverty.

## Academic secondary sources

Sugden's historical monograph is a canonical secondary source for chronology and source criticism [C2]. Begg's definitive history is another canonical synthesis, useful for distinguishing record from legend [C3]. Rumbelow's history is a standard reference but should be treated as canonical secondary rather than peer-reviewed criminology [C4]. Canter's investigative psychology and geographic profiling work supplies the spatial-inference vocabulary, not a solved identity [C5]. Rossmo supplies geographic profiling concepts [C6]. Cohen and Felson provide the routine activity model [C7]. Gangnam and Seoul CPTED sources support the contemporary grid translation [C8, C9]. Together these sources are sufficient for an educational YAML that avoids suspect claims and operational details.

## Behavioral analysis

### Victim profile

The profile should describe victim exposure in historical context: public-space vulnerability, economic precarity, night movement, and limited capable guardianship. It must not encode modern demographic targeting beyond "suitable target in low-guardian cell." Historical victims were real people, so the replay must avoid sensational detail and should not display their names in the viewer. Routine activity theory supports the simulation rule that convergence is situational: motivated actor, suitable target, and no capable guardian [C7].

### Modus operandi

The YAML should not reproduce injury detail. MO is translated into abstract movement: approach in low-light alley cell, brief convergence, immediate exit, and avoidance of guardian cells. Police files and historical monographs support that the canonical events occurred in public or semi-public East End locations, but exact interpretations differ [C1, C2]. The replay should represent only location and timing abstractions.

### Signature elements

No signature elements should be simulated. The Whitechapel case has a long record of speculation, and expressive details are often mythologized. The profile should contain a negative rule excluding graphic conduct, trophy behavior, letters, suspect theories, and medical-skill claims. This makes the overlay suitable for classroom discussion rather than true-crime dramatization.

### Geographic behavior

The geographic behavior should be local and path-sensitive. Canter and Rossmo's work can support discussion of anchor points, journey-to-crime, and distance decay [C5, C6]. The replay should use one historical-analogy anchor and a 2-4 cell operating radius around alley/commercial connectors. It should prefer back-alley cells and leave toward main-road edges after convergence. It must state that no case-solving geographic profile is encoded.

### Temporal patterns

The historical case is associated with night and early-morning public-space movement, but the replay's 7 p.m. time is a demonstration compromise for stakeholder viewing. The YAML should encode "low guardian density" and "transition period" rather than exact historical hours. Any temporal statement should be framed as scenario translation, not historical proof.

### Victim-offender interaction dynamics

Interaction should be modeled only as proximity and convergence. No dialogue, coercion, or graphic event is needed. The viewer can show "unguarded convergence" as an event type and then let faculty discuss how guardianship, street design, and routine movement affect risk.

## Geographic profiling data

Canter and Rossmo-related analyses of serial offending support the general idea that event locations can be evaluated for anchor points, buffer zones, and distance decay [C5, C6]. For the Whitechapel profile, the repository does not encode a measured centroid or suspect residence. It uses a synthetic local-radius parameter and a back-alley preference. This is a conscious ethical choice because Ripperology often overstates speculative solutions. The report should say that no named suspect, DNA claim, or final-solution book is used.

## Contemporary Seoul translation

Whitechapel alley density maps to Gangnam commercial back-alleys only as an urban-form analogy. It does not claim that Gangnam has comparable crime conditions. Gangnam literature supports the idea of a modern commercial district shaped by large-scale planning, superblocks, and material urban culture [C8]. Seoul CPTED research supports the role of lighting, CCTV, maintenance, and walking behavior in fear-of-crime outcomes [C9]. In the viewer, alleys are connector cells, main roads are visible guardian corridors, and commercial cells are high-flow areas. The profile should become less active where guardian markers overlap.

## Ethical considerations

The case is heavily mythologized. The profile must separate archival record from Ripperology speculation. It must not include suspect names, victim names, graphic details, letters as authentic behavior, or claims about medical skill unless a future user adds a sourced teaching note. The historical victims are not living, but their dignity still matters. The profile is permitted only because the offender is unidentified, the case is public-domain historical canon, and the YAML is abstract and pedagogical.

Implementation should make the difference between history and replay explicit. The profile should not say that Gangnam is Whitechapel, or that modern Seoul has the same risks as Victorian London. It should say that a dense alley network is being used as a visual analogy for discussing guardianship. The viewer should show the profile as an outlined triangle and should not alter the base 1000-agent replay. This separation lets presenters remove the overlay instantly if the audience wants to inspect the neutral city simulation.

The research note also needs a Ripperology boundary. Many books and articles argue for particular suspects, but the profile must not encode any suspect theory. The permitted behavior rules are case-agnostic abstractions: local anchor, alley preference, low guardian sensitivity, and immediate exit. Even those rules should be phrased as "teaching parameters." The profile should not use letters, nicknames beyond the display name required for historical recognition, or dramatic claims. If a source dispute exists, the profile should choose the less sensational phrasing.

For classroom use, this profile is best paired with a discussion of archival uncertainty. Police records, newspapers, memoirs, and later histories do not have equal evidentiary value. The viewer can help by showing literature sources and an ethical note in the profile panel. Instructors can then ask which pieces of behavior are actually supported by sources and which are only simulation assumptions. That is the constructive use of a famous historical case: it teaches caution about evidence, narrative, and the limits of inference.

The profile also must avoid operational path detail. It may move through alley cells, but it should not teach evasion, concealment, or attack procedure. It should generate only convergence and withdrawal states. This keeps the file within the repository's public-safety and academic framing.

The profile should be removed from any demo where the audience expects practical safety advice rather than historical-method discussion. Its purpose is source criticism, geographic analogy, and visibility of guardianship. The committed YAML should therefore keep the display copy restrained, keep the source list visible, and treat every behavior rule as a classroom assumption that can be challenged.

The ethical default is restraint whenever the archive is ambiguous.

That restraint should be visible in every viewer label.
